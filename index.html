CH 1

What is accounting?

Accounting turns business transactions into information people use to make decisions.

1. Interpret & recordWrite down each business transaction
→
2. ClassifyGroup similar transactions together
→
3. Summarize & communicateProduce reports for decision makers
External users (outside the company)

Owners, creditors, potential investors, unions, government agencies, suppliers, customers, the public.

They read the 4 financial statements: Balance sheet, Income statement, Statement of retained earnings, Statement of cash flows.

Internal users (inside the company)

Board of directors, CEO, CFO, vice presidents, business-unit, plant and store managers, supervisors.

The CFO oversees the Controller (accounting) and the Treasurer (cash & financing).

Goal of external reporting: help people make investment and credit decisions, judge the amount, timing and uncertainty of future cash flows, and see the company's resources and claims on them.

Memory trick "DEA-LER": Dividends, Expenses, Assets go up with a debit. Liabilities, Equity, Revenues go up with a credit. Debit = left side, credit = right side.

The 5 steps to record a transaction

1Identify the accounts and their type (A, L, E)
2Increase or decrease? Apply the debit/credit rules
3Write the entry in the journal (date order)
4Post it to the ledger (one page per account)
5Check that A = L + E still balances

Debit always written first; the credit line is indented.

Key principles

Time period principle: measure income over short, equal periods.

Matching / revenue recognition: record revenue when goods are sold or services done; record expenses in the period they helped earn revenue.

Accrual basis: what matters is when it is earned or used, not when cash moves.

Retained earnings = all profits since the start − all dividends paid.

Trial balance check: lists every account balance (assets first, then liabilities, then equity). If debits ≠ credits: look for a missing account, divide the difference by 2 (an amount put on the wrong side), divide it by 9 (digits swapped, e.g. 85 written 58). A balanced trial balance can still hide errors, such as using the wrong account.
CH 4

Adjusting entries

At the end of each period, fix the accounts so revenues and expenses land in the right period. This was Exercise 2 of your midterm (20 pts). The Chapter 4 slides weren't uploaded, so this summary follows your midterm.

Two rules: an adjusting entry never touches Cash, and it always pairs one income statement account with one balance sheet account.
1 · Prepaid expense: asset → expense

Paid 3,600 on Oct 1 for one year of insurance, debited to Prepaid insurance. One month = 3,600 ÷ 12 = 300.

Dr Insurance expense 300 · Cr Prepaid insurance 300

2 · Unearned revenue: liability → revenue

Received 1,200 on Dec 1 for 3 months of services, credited to Unearned revenue. December earned = 1,200 ÷ 3 = 400.

Dr Unearned revenue 400 · Cr Service revenue 400

3 · Accrued expense: used, not yet paid

Salaries 1,500/week (5 days) = 300/day. Last payday Friday Dec 26; Dec 29, 30, 31 unpaid = 3 days → 900.

Dr Salaries expense 900 · Cr Salaries payable 900

4 · Accrued revenue: earned, not yet billed

Services done in December, not billed or recorded: 800.

Dr Accounts receivable 800 · Cr Service revenue 800

5 · Depreciation: asset → expense (an example of type 1)

Machine 18,000, 4-year life, adjusted every month: 18,000 ÷ (4 × 12) = 375 per month.

Dr Depreciation expense 375 · Cr Accumulated depreciation 375

Read the frequency. "The company records adjusting entries at the end of each month": the December entry covers one month only, because earlier months were already adjusted. "Only once a year": count all the months since the start (insurance bought Oct 1 → 3 months = 900).

CH 7

Financial assets

Cash and anything that turns easily into a known amount of cash: short-term investments and receivables.

Cash

Coins, notes, cheques, card sales, money orders: anything the bank accepts as a deposit.

Cash equivalents

Very safe, stable value, matures within 90 days. E.g. money-market funds, Treasury bills.

Restricted cash / credit line

Restricted cash can't be used for daily needs. A line of credit only becomes a liability once you draw on it.

Internal control over cash: the person who handles cash never keeps the records · deposit receipts daily · avoid paying in cash (except petty cash) · separate approving from paying · prepare cash budgets · reconcile the bank statement promptly.

When prices are rising

FIFO: lowest COGS, so the highest profit (and higher taxes). Inventory is valued at recent costs.

LIFO: highest COGS, so the lowest profit, the most conservative method. Inventory is valued at old costs. IFRS prohibits LIFO.

Specific ID: logical when units are unique (cars, art).

Other rules

Consistency: keep the same method every year. You can change it only if you explain why and disclose the effects.

Physical count: adjusts the records for shrinkage (theft, breakage, spoilage). Obsolete goods are written down to zero or scrap value. In both cases: Dr COGS / Cr Inventory.

On the balance sheet, inventory comes right after accounts receivable.

CH 9

Plant & intangible assets

Long-lived assets are spread as an expense over the years they help earn revenue (matching principle).

Tangible

Depreciation

Buildings, equipment, furniture, land improvements. Land is never depreciated.

Intangible

Amortization

Patents, copyrights, goodwill. No physical substance, often exclusive rights.

Natural resources

Depletion

Oil, timber, minerals. Dr Depletion expense / Cr Natural resources.

What goes into the cost?

Purchase price + everything reasonable and necessary to get the asset to its location and ready to use: transport, insurance in transit, taxes, installation, testing, legal fees, clearing land.

Lump-sum purchase: split the total by relative market values.

Spending after purchase

Capital expenditure increases capacity, efficiency or useful life → added to the asset.

Revenue expenditure ordinary maintenance → expense now.

Two depreciation methods: S&G truck (cost 17,000, residual 2,000, 5 years)

Straight-line

(17,000 − 2,000) ÷ 5 = 3,000 / year

Same expense every year.

Double-declining balance

Book value × (1/5 × 2) = BV × 40%

Year 1: 17,000 × 40% = 6,800. Ignore residual value until the last year, where expense = BV − residual (2,203 − 2,000 = 203).

Straight-line book valueDouble-declining book value
Journal entry & where it shows

Dr Depreciation expense 3,000 → income statement

Cr Accumulated depreciation 3,000 → balance sheet, contra-asset

Book value = Cost − Accumulated depreciation

Special cases

Partial year: bought July 1 → 6/12 of a year. Half-month rule: bought on/before the 15th counts the full month.

Revised estimate: (BV now − residual) ÷ remaining life. Truck after 3 years: (8,000 − 2,000) ÷ 5 = 1,200/yr.

Consistency: don't switch methods from year to year.

Disposal (expert part): compare cash received with book value

Cash > BV → Gain

Sell truck (BV 2,000) for 3,000: gain 1,000 (credit).

Cash < BV → Loss

Sell for 1,000: loss 1,000 (debit). Discard for 0: loss 2,000.

Cash = BV

No gain or loss.

Steps: ① update depreciation to date ② remove the asset (Cr cost) and its accumulated depreciation (Dr) ③ record cash ④ plug the gain or loss.

CLASS 1

Introduction to AI

What intelligence is, the history of AI in summers and winters, the two paradigms, and how humans and machines learn from data.

Definition attempt 1

Technologies that, by imitating beings considered intelligent, attempt to deal with computationally intractable problems.

Definition attempt 2

Systems that exhibit intelligent behaviour by analysing their environment and executing actions, with some autonomy, to achieve given objectives.

Intelligence is "a semantic mess": awareness, intentionality, creativity, understanding, learning. Searle's Chinese Room (1980): a system can manipulate symbols correctly without understanding them.

Timeline: AI summers and winters

1940–1956Birth of AI

1943 McCulloch-Pitts neuron · 1950 Von Neumann & Turing move to binary logic · 1951 SNARC (Minsky & Edmonds), first analog neural network · 1956 Dartmouth Conference: AI is born (symbolic only, no neural networks)

1956–1974Golden age ☀

Over-optimistic predictions (Simon, Minsky) · 1957 Mark I Perceptron (man vs woman images, first computer vision) · 1960 MADALINE, first NN on a real problem · weights set by hand

1974–1980First winter ❄

1966 ALPAC report on machine translation · 1969 "Perceptrons" book: perceptrons cannot learn XOR, NN funding cut · 1973 Lighthill report (combinatorial explosion) · DARPA frustration

1980–1987Renaissance ☀

Expert systems (inference engine mimicking a human expert): DENDRAL (chemistry), MYCIN (blood diseases) · backpropagation described in 1970, first efficient NN use ~1982

1987–1993Second winter ❄

Expert systems costly, depend on human experts, can't generalize · 1987 LISP machine market collapses · Deep Blue beats Kasparov (1997) but funding doesn't return

2010–todayAI boom ☀

Big data + deep learning (subsymbolic) + GPUs (Moore's law, NVIDIA, AMD) · LLMs, diffusion, self-driving, AlphaFold, robotics…

Symbolic AI (GOFAI, 1950s–80s)

Explicit knowledge: symbols + rules + logic. Predicate logic, semantic networks (knowledge graphs), rule-based systems ("if it rains then WetFloor"), theorem provers (Isabelle).

Weak handling uncertainty, learning from experience · combinatorial explosion

Subsymbolic AI (today)

Raw data + neural networks. Knowledge is learned, not written by hand.

Strong perception, patterns, learning from data

Moravec's paradox (1988): what is easy for a human (seeing a cup, walking) is hard for a computer, and what is hard for a human (chess, theorems) is easy.
Deduction

Rule + fact → certain conclusion. Infallible if the rules are true.

Induction

Many observations → general rule. Fallible.

Abduction

Best explanation of what you see (the scientific method). Can fail. This is what ML does.

Probabilities

Independent events: P(X and Y) = P(X) × P(Y). Two sixes: 1/6 × 1/6 = 1/36.

In general: P(X and Y) = P(X) × P(Y|X). Knowing only P(rain) and P(wet floor) is not enough: you need more data.

Machine learning in one line

Collect representative data, then find the model that makes your data most likely: maximize P(data | model), the likelihood.

Confirmation bias (the 3-number game): we look for examples that confirm our rule. To test a hypothesis you need cases for and against.

Linear regression

y = b0 + b1·x

Example: Tcore = 2 × Toutside + 350. Learning = finding b0 and b1 that minimize the MSE (set the partial derivatives to 0).

MSE = average of (y − ŷ)²

Over- and underfitting

Underfitting = you didn't study: bad on train and test. Overfitting = you memorised last year's exam: perfect on train, fails on new data.

Accuracy = (TP + TN) ÷ total: share of all predictions that are right.

Precision = TP ÷ (TP + FP): of what I called positive, how much really is?

Recall = TP ÷ (TP + FN): of the real positives, how many did I find?

F1 = 2 × P × R ÷ (P + R): the balance between the two.

Watch the cat example in the slides. 50 cats and 50 dogs; 42 cats are found, 8 cats are called "dog", 35 dogs are right and 15 dogs are called "cat". With "cat" as the positive class, the standard definitions give TP 42, FN 8, FP 15, TN 35. The slide prints FP = 8 and FN = 15, which looks swapped, so check with your professor. With the standard reading: accuracy 77%, precision 42/57 = 73.7%, recall 42/50 = 84%.
CLASS 3

Deep learning

From one neuron to deep networks: why activation functions matter, and how networks learn with backpropagation and gradient descent.

The perceptron (neuron)

a = f(w1·x1 + w2·x2 + w3·x3 + b)

Without f, a neuron is a linear classifier: it can do AND and OR, but never XOR.

Why activation functions?

Stacking linear neurons still gives a linear function, however many you add. The activation function makes the network non-linear, so it can learn any boundary: neural networks are universal function approximators.

Step0 or 1
ReLUmax(0, z), hidden layers
Sigmoid0 to 1: binary output
Softmaxprobabilities summing to 1: multi-class output
1. Forward passinput goes through the network to an output
→
2. Losscompare with the label: MSE (regression), binary cross-entropy, cross-entropy
→
3. Backpropagationpartial derivative of the loss for every weight (chain rule)
→
4. Gradient descentmove all weights a small step downhill, then repeat
Learning rate

Size of each step in the loss landscape. Too big: you jump over the minimum. Too small: very slow.

Epoch · batch · step

Epoch = all the data used once. Batch = subset used for one update. Step = one batch → gradient → update.

SGD

Stochastic gradient descent estimates the gradient on a random batch: less memory, and it helps against overfitting.

Spotting overfitting

A gap between training and validation curves. More neurons and layers = more expressive = easier to overfit.

Fixing overfitting

Regularization: penalty on large weights in the loss (L2). Dropout: randomly switch off neurons. Batch normalization: normalize each layer's inputs per mini-batch, then scale (γ) and shift (β); nobody fully knows why it works. Convolutional layers (Class 6).

A tensor is a multi-dimensional array (a generalized matrix). Tools: Keras/TensorFlow and PyTorch. MNIST: 60,000 training and 10,000 test images of handwritten digits.

CLASS 5

Unsupervised learning

No labels: the algorithm finds structure by itself. Two families: clustering and dimensionality reduction.

K-means
  1. Choose k.
  2. Place k centroids.
  3. Assign each point to the nearest centroid.
  4. Move each centroid to the mean of its points.
  5. Repeat until nothing changes.

Minimizes inertia = sum of squared distances to the assigned centroid.

Pro simple, fast · Con sensitive to the starting centroids, you must choose k

DBSCAN

Clusters = dense regions. Two parameters: Eps (radius) and MinPts. Points are core (dense region), border, or noise (outliers).

Pro robust to outliers, finds the number of clusters itself · Con sensitive to its parameters, expensive

Mall customers example (k = 5)

Clusters by annual income × spending score: low/high, medium/medium, high/low, high/high, low/low. Age did not form distinct groups. Uses: customer segmentation, image segmentation, anomaly detection.

PCA (linear)
  1. Standardize features (mean 0, variance 1).
  2. Covariance matrix.
  3. Eigenvalues and eigenvectors = directions of highest variance.
  4. Keep the top k = principal components.
t-SNE (non-linear, for visualizing)

Keeps local neighbourhoods. Similarities in high dimension (Gaussian) vs low dimension (Student's t), then gradient descent moves points: similar ones attract, different ones repel. Great for showing MNIST clusters in 2D.

Computer vision

Dense networks flatten the image and lose local patterns, so they fail on CIFAR-10. Convolutional neural networks (CNNs) fix that.

Kernel

A 2D neuron whose weights are learned by backprop. A 3×3 kernel turns each 3×3 patch into 1 value. Padding (a border of zeros) keeps the output the same size.

Pooling (subsampling)

1. Reduces dimensions and overfitting. 2. Translation invariance. 3. Hierarchical features, from edges to objects.

Data augmentation

Rotations, shifts, zoom, flips… more and more varied data, closer to the real world.

MilestoneWhy it matters

Fine-tuning

Start from a network already trained on a big dataset (ImageNet) and adapt it to your small dataset: more efficient and more accurate than training from scratch.

Explainability & bias

Grad-CAM, Grad-CAM++ and LRP show which pixels drove a decision, which can reveal a model using the wrong clue (bias).

CLASS 7

Reinforcement learning

An agent learns by trial and error from rewards, with no labels.

The elements

State = full description of the world; observation = the partial part the agent sees. Action space: discrete or continuous. Reward: the only feedback. Policy π: the agent's brain, state → action.

Reward hypothesis: every goal can be described as maximizing the expected cumulative reward (the expected return).

Discounted return

G = R1 + γ·R2 + γ²·R3 + …

γ (gamma, between 0 and 1) makes far-away, uncertain rewards count less.

Exploration vs exploitation

Take the small, sure reward you know (exploit) or try new actions that might lead to bigger rewards (explore)?

Policy-basedValue-based (Q-learning)
Learnsthe policy directly: state → action, or a probability over actions (e.g. rock 50%, paper 30%, scissors 20%)a value function; the policy is "go to the highest-value state"
HowGradient ascent on the expected reward: make good actions more likelyBellman equation: V(St) = Rt+1 + γ·V(St+1). Q(s, a) = value of action a in state s
Explorationimplicit, through the stochastic policysometimes try actions with uncertain or lower Q-values
Monte Carlo tree search

Selection → Expansion → Simulation (random playout to the end) → Backpropagation of the result up the path. Problem: Go has ~2.1×10¹⁷⁰ positions (vs ~10⁸⁰ atoms in the universe).

AlphaGo = deep RL

3 networks: a fast policy network, a strong policy network (13-layer CNN), and a value network.

Human games → policy network → self-play with rewards ±1 → policy optimization → value network from self-play games → tree search guided by the policy and value networks.

Multi-agent systems: decentralized (other agents are part of the environment) or centralized (one policy for all). Self-play uses past copies of the agent as opponents; skill is tracked with an ELO rating. Also: MuZero (plans without knowing the rules), RLHF, agentic frameworks.

CLASS 8

Generative AI

Models that learn the patterns of existing data and generate new examples that resemble it.

GANs

First breakthrough in image generation.

Diffusion models

State of the art for images, video and audio: DALL-E, Stable Diffusion, Midjourney, Sora, MovieGen.

Transformers

State of the art for text: ChatGPT, Claude, PaLM.

GAN: two networks against each other

Random noise
→
Generatormakes fake examples
→
Discriminatorsees real + fake, guesses which is which
→
Both improveuntil the discriminator is stuck at 50% (chance)
Encoder–decoder

The encoder compresses a big input (MNIST: 784 dimensions) into a small vector, the embedding. The decoder rebuilds the input from it. They are trained together: if the reconstruction is good, the embedding kept the information.

Diffusion

Forward: add Gaussian noise step by step. Reverse: a noise predictor (a U-Net) learns to remove it, so you can generate from pure noise.

Latent diffusion (Stable Diffusion) works in the small latent space: much faster. Conditioning steers the result: text encoded (CLIP, transformer) and injected with attention, or an image added to the noise.

Transformers (2017, "Attention is all you need")

Attention tells the model which parts of the input matter most, based on similarity (dot product) between tokens.

Query (what I'm looking for) · Key (what I'm compared with) · Value (the context information). Q, K and V are learned.

Why they won

Blocks can be stacked without limit (1 trillion+ parameters, scaling laws: bigger is better). Trained on one task, predicting the next token, yet they solve any text task. Also works for images and video. Open question: are they just memory machines?

Applications of diffusion: synthetic data (e.g. medical images), audio generation.
